# House planning methods: zoning, circulation, adjacency, parti and typologies (for the BuildWise layout engine)

Scope note: residential, single-family. Sources are a mix of primary texts (Alexander's *A Pattern Language* patterns 127/131/132 as hosted online; House-GAN, GPLAN, Palladian and Prairie grammar papers), trade/practice journalism (Fine Homebuilding, JLC, Houzz) and design-education blogs (illustrarch, studiomatrx, Coohom). The blog sources are flagged where used; several (studiomatrx, illustrarch) carry odd or future dates and should be treated as illustrative, not authoritative. Neufert and Ching were not directly accessible online in this pass (see Gaps).

## 1. How architects zone a house, and the standard adjacency matrices / bubble diagrams

### Takeaway
Houses are zoned into public (guests welcome), private (family/sleeping) and service (kitchen, WC, utility) areas arranged along an "intimacy gradient" from the entrance (most public) to bedrooms (most private); bathrooms sit between common and private zones. Adjacency is recorded as a pairwise matrix (essential / desirable / neutral / must-separate) and drawn as a bubble diagram before any walls; strong pairs include kitchen-dining and bedroom-bathroom.

### Cited Findings
- Most houses break into three zones: public ("areas where guests are welcome"), private ("areas reserved for the family") and service (supporting the house). Example programme tagging: entry, living, dining = public; bedrooms, bathroom, balcony = private; kitchen and WC = service. — [illustrarch, Bubble diagram residential layout](https://illustrarch.com/articles/architectural-diagrams/98402-bubble-diagram-residential-layout.html)
- Recommended first step: three or four large zone circles, public zone near the entry, private zone toward the quiet edge; every room is tagged with a zone before drawing. The entry "filters guests away from private zones". — [illustrarch](https://illustrarch.com/articles/architectural-diagrams/98402-bubble-diagram-residential-layout.html)
- Strong (solid-line, daily) adjacencies: kitchen-dining; bedroom-bathroom (a heavier line for a direct, step-free path from the primary bedroom to its bathroom). Weaker/occasional (dashed): garage-mudroom, office-entry. Rooms that must stay apart are left unconnected and drawn distant. Powder room, pantry and closet are treated as small support circles. — [illustrarch](https://illustrarch.com/articles/architectural-diagrams/98402-bubble-diagram-residential-layout.html)
- "Circulation is a space too": houses that ignore it leave rooms reachable only through other rooms. Mark the front door, the main circulation spine and secondary access (garage/rear door). — [illustrarch](https://illustrarch.com/articles/architectural-diagrams/98402-bubble-diagram-residential-layout.html)
- Orientation in zoning: morning rooms lean east; living spaces favour afternoon/evening light; street side, views and slope pull rooms to particular edges. — [illustrarch](https://illustrarch.com/articles/architectural-diagrams/98402-bubble-diagram-residential-layout.html)
- Adjacency matrix scales used in teaching: 2 = essential, 1 = desirable, 0 = none, "−" = separate; or essential / desirable / neutral / must-separate, listed with each space's area. Zoning axes to sort the programme before drawing walls: public/private, served/servant, wet/dry, active/quiet. — summarised from [CUNY City Tech OpenLab programming assignment](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/arch3612f23/assignments/assignment-programing/) and [illustrarch zoning diagrams](https://illustrarch.com/articles/architectural-diagrams/75204-architectural-zoning-diagrams.html) (via search summary; pages not opened in full)
- Alexander, Pattern 127 Intimacy Gradient: a house needs a graded sequence of settings; the entrance/porch is most public, then common area and kitchen, then study/back sitting room, with bedroom most intimate. Sequence given: gate, porch, entrance, sitting wall, common space and kitchen, private garden, bed alcoves. Common areas sit at the heart of the home and all paths to more private rooms pass alongside them. Bathing rooms and toilets sit halfway between common and private areas so both can reach them. — [Pattern 127 (patterns-dev)](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl127/apl127.htm); [urbigenous pattern 127](https://urbigenous.net/a-pattern-language/pattern-127.html) (via search summary)
- Cultural strength of the gradient: Alexander notes in Peruvian houses some guests never get past the sala, some never past the front threshold — i.e. a formal reception room near the door is a real requirement in some cultures. — [Pattern 127](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl127/apl127.htm) (via search summary)
- Dwelling-level zoning type: "Zoning" separates the living zone from the bedroom zone, "often with a separate corridor in each". — [De Gruyter Building Types, A Taxonomy of Typological Classifications](https://bdt.degruyter.com/a-taxonomy-of-typological-classifications/)
- Vertical zoning on narrow multi-storey plots: ground = parking, utility, stair foot, store; lower-mid = entry, formal living, kitchen, guest WC; middle = daily living/dining; upper = bedrooms, study; roof = skylight, solar, tanks, garden. "The higher you go, the more daylight, the more privacy from the street, and the quieter it gets." — [studiomatrx, Narrow plot design strategies (India)](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/narrow-plot-design-strategies-india) (blog; illustrative)

### Inferences
- Engine rule candidates: assign every room a zone (`public | private | service`) and an intimacy rank (entrance 0, living/dining/kitchen 1, study/family 2, bedrooms 3). Score a plan by whether the graph distance from the entrance increases monotonically with rank, and penalise any path from the entrance to a public room that passes through a private room.
- Guest WC: the sources consistently put toilets "halfway" between common and private zones (Alexander) and class WC as service (illustrarch). Combined with "entry filters guests away from private zones", the guest WC should be reachable from the entrance/hall without crossing the living room or the bedroom zone.
- A minimal house adjacency matrix the engine could encode (essential = must share a wall or opening): kitchen–dining (essential), dining–living (essential or open), entrance–living (essential), master bedroom–ensuite (essential, direct), bedrooms–family bathroom (desirable, via bedroom lobby), kitchen–service entrance/utility/store (desirable), entrance–guest WC (desirable), bedrooms–kitchen/entrance (separate).

### Gaps
- Neufert's room-relationship diagrams (functional flow charts for dwellings) and Ching's adjacency examples could not be fetched online; no verified numeric adjacency weights from those books.
- No source found giving a standard "rule" for the service entrance location beyond the kitchen–utility/garage–mudroom links above.

## 2. Circulation patterns for houses and why long corridors are poor

### Takeaway
The canonical guidance (Alexander 131/132) is to avoid corridors where possible: let public/common rooms carry movement in a chain or loop with private rooms opening off them, and keep any unavoidable passage short (under ~50 ft / 15 m), daylit, ideally on one side of the building, and furnished like a room. Double-loaded corridors are singled out as disorienting; circulation through rooms or galleries uses less area. No authoritative housing standard publishes a single-family circulation percentage; trade rules of thumb cluster at ~10–12% of floor area.

### Cited Findings
- Pattern 131 The Flow Through Rooms: "As far as possible, avoid the use of corridors and passages." Use public and common rooms for both movement and gathering, arranged "in a chain or loop" so people walk from room to room, with private rooms opening directly off the public ones. Ideally "a loop, which passes through all the major rooms, public and common". Rooms used for movement should have more than one door (doors placed in the corners of rooms), plenty of light, and be furnished. If passages are unavoidable, make them wide and bright, "ideally on one side of the building". — [Pattern 131 (patterns-dev)](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl131/apl131.htm)
- Pattern 131 reasoning: "The movement between rooms is as important as the rooms themselves"; dark, narrow passages with rooms opening as dead ends make movement an ordeal and discourage informal contact; a loop "ties the rooms together". — [Pattern 131](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl131/apl131.htm)
- Pattern 132 Short Passages: "long, sterile corridors set the scene for everything bad about modern architecture"; they "represent bureaucracy and monotony". An earlier version of the pattern gives "a figure of some 50 feet as a critical threshold" beyond which "passages begin to feel dead and monotonous"; longer passages must have their scale broken down (lit in patches from one side, or opening into wider rooms at intervals). Evidence cited: Spivack (1967) on long hospital corridors — distorted perception of distance, faces and time, poor acoustics, increased anxiety. Solution: "Keep passages short. Make them as much like rooms as possible, with carpets or wood on the floor, furniture, bookshelves, beautiful windows"; best passages have "windows along an entire wall"; interior windows into adjoining rooms "help animate the hall". — [Pattern 132 (patterns-dev)](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl132/apl132.htm)
- Fine Homebuilding ("Houses that Flow"): linear circulation suits elongated plans; through-room (axial) circulation is the most space-efficient form and feels more intimate; single-loaded corridors cost more area but give privacy; double-loaded corridors "can feel disorienting" — open the walls between hall and rooms to counter this. "A corridor with light and views is inviting." Give every path a window on at least one side. Avoid dead ends: rooms ideally have at least two entrances so traffic can loop. Target-style circulation puts closets, fireplaces and stairs in a central core with communal rooms in the outer ring; perimeter circulation keeps living spaces free of through-traffic at the cost of a longer route. Enfilade (aligned doorways) frames views down a path. — [Fine Homebuilding, Drawing Board: Houses that Flow](https://finehomebuilding.com/?p=44009)
- Dwelling access types catalogued by De Gruyter Building Types: Corridor/Hallway (rooms in sequence on one or both sides of a linear circulation space); Zoning (living zone vs bedroom zone, each with its own corridor); Inserted Core (a core organises the plan, other space left open); Living Room as Circulation Center ("the intersection of all routes"); Circular Path (continuous route through most rooms, rooms often with more than one entrance); Open Plan. — [De Gruyter Building Types taxonomy](https://bdt.degruyter.com/a-taxonomy-of-typological-classifications/)
- JLC: a gallery (a hallway without walls) improves circulation where a hallway is not needed for privacy. — [JLC, Troubleshooting Open Plans](https://www.jlconline.com/projects/troubleshooting-open-plans_o/) (via search summary)
- Building Advisor: a "linked room" plan (circulation through rooms, no dedicated hall) gives intimacy but poor circulation. — [Building Advisor, Circulation: key to a successful floor plan](https://buildingadvisor.com/design/floor-plans/circulation-key-to-a-successful-floor-plan) (via search summary)
- Circulation share, examples: a 2,815 sq ft home with ~924 sq ft (~32%) in hallway described as wasteful; a 2,588 sq ft four-bedroom plan with ~115 sq ft of circulation (~4%). — [GardenWeb/Houzz, Do You Really Need That Hallway?](https://www.gardenweb.com/ideabooks/17320457/list/do-you-really-need-that-hallway) (via search summary)
- Rules of thumb (low-rigor, unsourced): keep hallways under ~10% of floor area ([Coohom, corridor and circulation in narrow homes](https://www.coohom.com/article/how-to-optimize-corridor-and-circulation-space-in-narrow-homes)); ~12% ceiling for a 2,150 sq ft house ([Coohom, 2150 sq ft mistakes](https://www.coohom.com/article/common-floor-plan-mistakes-when-designing-a-2150-sq-ft-house)). Both via search summary.
- Corridor dimension guidance from housing guides: avoid excessively long corridors with changes of direction, recessed entries and break-out zones; widths ≥1500 mm in high-density buildings, 1000–1200 mm in low-density ones (Housing Choices Australia 2024 guide); NJIT checklist flags unbroken corridors over ~100 ft (~30 m). These are multi-unit/building corridors, not in-dwelling halls. — [HCA Design Guidelines 2024](https://online.flippingbook.com/view/417645034/21/); [NJIT design checklist](https://web.njit.edu/abs/check/check_buildlay_06.html) (via search summary)
- UK Nationally Described Space Standard counts stair circulation within gross internal area but sets no circulation percentage. — [Gov.uk NDSS](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/technical-housing-standards-nationally-described-space-standard/technical-housing-standards-nationally-described-space-standard) (via search summary)

### Inferences
- Engine rule candidates to kill "barracks" plans:
  1. Prefer hub circulation: entrance → hall/lobby → living (hub) with dining/kitchen in a chain, private rooms off a short bedroom lobby. Treat a dedicated corridor as a cost, not a default.
  2. Hard cap a single straight corridor run in a house at ~15 m (Alexander's 50 ft) and soft-penalise from much lower (a bedroom lobby serving 3–4 doors rarely needs >4–6 m — this figure is an inference, not sourced).
  3. Penalise double-loaded runs where both sides are bedrooms for more than ~2 doors per side; favour a lobby (a wider node with doors around its perimeter) over a linear corridor.
  4. Every corridor/passage should touch an external wall (window on at least one side, Fine Homebuilding; "on one side of the building", Alexander) — a corridor buried in the middle of a double-loaded plan fails this.
  5. Circulation area target ≤10–12% of gross floor area (trade rule of thumb, weakly sourced), and flag >20% as a defect.
  6. Reward loops through public rooms (rooms with ≥2 doors) and penalise dead-end public rooms.

### Gaps
- No authoritative (code or government guide) percentage for in-dwelling circulation in single-family houses was found; the 10–12% figures come from low-rigor web content.
- The London Housing Design Standards LPG (2023) was returned by search but not opened; it may contain dwelling-entrance and circulation rules ([GLA LPG PDF](https://www.london.gov.uk/media/8395/download)).

## 3. The parti and common house typologies

### Takeaway
The parti (from French *parti pris*, "to make a decision", Beaux-Arts origin) is the single organising idea of a building expressed as a one-line diagram, e.g. "rooms around a courtyard". House typologies (courtyard/atrium, L, U, split-level, row house, linked/pavilion, cluster) are effectively reusable partis, each matched to site, climate and privacy conditions.

### Cited Findings
- Parti: the main organising concept behind a building, usually captured in one simple diagram; from French *parti pris*; also linked to *prendre parti* ("to make a decision") and the 19th-century École des Beaux-Arts. Examples: a diagram of "rooms around a courtyard"; a house that becomes transparent and frames views. Parti diagrams are "one-liners" illustrating a single idea; the concept is the idea and the parti its diagram. — [Wikipedia, Parti (architecture)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parti_(architecture)); [illustrarch, What is a parti diagram](https://illustrarch.com/articles/architectural-diagrams/27049-what-is-architectural-parti-diagram.html); [Houzz, What's the Big Idea?](https://www.houzz.com.au/magazine/what-s-the-big-idea-in-architecture-it-s-the-parti-pris-stsetivw-vs~7867799) (via search summary)
- Geometric organisations catalogued: Centralized, Linear, Radial, Cluster, Grid, Complex. Form types: Row House ("rather deep but narrow, laid out between two long party walls and often two levels high"); Courtyard House and Atrium House (atrium centre "as a shared space or for daylighting"); Split-Level ("staggered in predominantly half-level increments"); Clustered Low-Rise/Mat; Detached. "Split-level layouts are often used on narrow building lots, such as those lending themselves to row housing." Corner buildings suffer "the poorly lit inside corner". — [De Gruyter Building Types taxonomy](https://bdt.degruyter.com/a-taxonomy-of-typological-classifications/)
- Courtyard houses are introverted with few openings outward; the courtyard often serves as the central circulation route; in northern climates relying only on the open court for access is impractical; in desert climates a courtyard pool cools the microclimate. — [McGill eScholarship thesis](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/x059c9526.pdf); [Houzz, Light and private courtyard houses](https://www.houzz.com.sg/magazine/great-compositions-light-and-private-courtyard-houses-stsetivw-vs~938672) (via search summary)
- L-plan: addresses privacy and noise in crowded areas, defines a courtyard for garden/water/outdoor kitchen, works for big houses on sloped or narrow lots; can be split-level or multi-storey. — [The Plan Collection, 4 advantages of L-shaped plans](https://www.theplancollection.com/blog/4-advantages-of-l-shaped-house-plans-and-how-they-solve-common-problems) (plan seller; via search summary)
- U-plan: wraps a patio/courtyard on three sides for a sheltered outdoor room; attractive where space and privacy are scarce; suits multigenerational living. — [The Plan Collection, U-shaped advantages](https://theplancollection.com/blog/advantages-of-u-shaped-house-design) (via search summary)
- Split-level: two nearly equal sections on different levels joined by a short stair; on slopes, terraced houses become split-level with spatial hierarchies between street and garden. — [Detail, low-rise housing typology](https://detail.de/de_en/low-rise-housing-typology-14520) (via search summary)
- Prairie House grammar (Koning & Eizenberg 1981): generation starts from the fireplace — "the establishment of a fireplace is the key to defining the prairie-style house" — then adds rectangular, Froebel-like blocks in sequence, with exterior details last. Corpus reported as 11 houses (Winslow to Robie) by one source, 13 houses built 1900–1909 by another (conflict). Critique: captures formal properties but ignores socio-functional ones. Knight extended it to Usonian houses. — [Koning & Eizenberg, EPB 8(3) 1981](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/b080295); [CMU lecture notes, Prairie Houses](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/lecture/PrairieHouses.pdf) (via search summary)

### Inferences
- For BuildWise, "concept" should be chosen before rooms: pick a parti (hub house, L around a court, U around a court, linked pavilions, courtyard/atrium, split-level, row/narrow) from plot shape, climate and privacy, then derive zones and circulation from it. This mirrors the Prairie grammar's "anchor first" (fireplace) and RPLAN's "living room first" (Section 4).
- Linked pavilions / L-plans naturally keep corridors short because each wing is a single zone (day wing, night wing) joined at a hub; this directly addresses the barracks failure.
- In hot-humid climates (Nigeria, the worked example), courtyards and L/U forms add cross-ventilation and shaded outdoor rooms; the courtyard can be the circulation (verandah gallery) instead of an internal corridor. (Inference from the courtyard sources above; no Nigeria-specific source in this pass.)

### Gaps
- No good source fetched on "pavilion / linked pavilion" or "bar house" as named house typologies, nor decision rules for choosing between them; Ching's *Form, Space and Order* organisations chapter would be the canonical reference but was not accessed.

## 4. How generative design research represents and generates good house plans

### Takeaway
Research represents a house as an adjacency/connectivity graph of typed rooms (bubble diagram) plus a boundary, then realises it geometrically by grammar rules (Palladian, Prairie), graph-theoretic rectangular duals (GPLAN), stochastic optimisation of a cost function (Merrell et al.), or learned generators (House-GAN, RPLAN/Graph2Plan). Known failure modes — inaccessible rooms, missing doors, bad proportions — come from treating adjacency as geometry without modelling doors/circulation.

### Cited Findings
- House-GAN (ECCV 2020): input is a bubble diagram as a graph — nodes are rooms with types (living room, kitchen, bedroom, bathroom, closet, balcony, corridor, dining room, laundry room, unknown), edges are spatial adjacency (two rooms connected if the Manhattan distance between bounding boxes < 8 px). Output: axis-aligned room bounding boxes. Data: 117,587 plans from LIFULL HOME's (~5 million plans), rescaled to 256×256, grouped by room count 1–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12, 13+. Metrics: realism (user study incl. professional architects), diversity (FID), compatibility (graph edit distance between input and extracted graph). Limitations: no room size in nodes, rooms always rectangles, edges are adjacency not doors; failures include improper sizes/shapes, misalignment and inaccessible rooms (blocked entries). — [House-GAN, arXiv 2003.06988](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.06988)
- House-GAN++ (CVPR 2021): iterative refinement (previous layout becomes next constraint), non-rectangular rooms, generates doors/entrances, uses a functional (door-connectivity) graph instead of an adjacency graph. — [House-GAN++, arXiv 2103.02574](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.02574) (via search summary)
- RPLAN / Wu et al. 2019 (ACM TOG 38(6)): input is the building boundary; rooms placed first, then walls; reportedly begins by positioning the living room because it appears in almost all plans, then iteratively adds rooms; dataset of 80,788 real Asian residential plans. The living-room-first detail comes from a secondary summary. — [Project page](https://ustc-gcl-f.github.io/projects/DeepLayout/index.html); [aminer abstract](https://www.aminer.cn/pub/5dc69ae4df1a9c0c415209a6/data-driven-interior-plan-generation-for-residential-buildings) (via search summary); follow-up [Graph2Plan, arXiv 2004.13204](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.13204) learns plans from layout graphs plus boundary.
- Merrell, Schkufza & Koltun 2010 (ACM TOG 29, SIGGRAPH Asia): the architectural programme (rooms, sizes, adjacencies) is sampled from a Bayesian network trained on real residential layouts; the layout is then found by Metropolis-style stochastic search with moves such as sliding a wall, splitting a wall, snapping walls; the cost function includes accessibility and dimension terms (full formula not verified). Authors prioritise visual similarity to real layouts over strict functional performance. — [Princeton-hosted PDF](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spring11/cos598A/pdfs/Merrell10a.pdf); [Purdue SIGGRAPH 2011 course slides](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/urban/sg_2011_course/umc_SG11_03_computational_building_design.pdf) (via search summary; PDF fetch timed out)
- GPLAN: builds dimensioned floorplans from an adjacency graph using graph theory and optimisation; produces several topologically distinct plans inside a rectangular boundary. — [GPLAN, arXiv 2008.01803](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.01803) (via search summary)
- Rectangular duals: a rectangular dual may not exist for a given adjacency graph; known constructions cover planar triangulated graphs (Koźmiński & Kinnen; Bhasker & Sahni); no general existence test for arbitrary adjacency graphs per the authors. — [arXiv 1910.00081](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.00081); [Cambridge AI EDAM, minimum bends](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/214D14B8D2D263DE4B2D5C97103165F0/S0890060424000179a.pdf/automated-generation-of-floor-plans-with-minimum-bends.pdf) (via search summary)
- Palladian grammar (Stiny & Mitchell 1978, EPB 5:5–18): parametric shape grammar for villa ground plans; bilateral symmetry is the most conspicuous feature; plans are on labelled rectangular "tartan" grids, most on 5×3 grids; an eight-stage process (grid definition, exterior walls, room layout, entrances, etc.); generates Villa Malcontenta. Companion "Counting Palladian Plans" enumerates layouts. — [Stiny & Mitchell PDF (CMU)](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/ramesh/teaching/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Stiny&MItchell-1978-EPB5_5-18.ThePalladianGrammar.pdf); [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1068/b050005) (via search summary)
- Survey framing: traditional layout generation is "based on optimization techniques or heuristic design guidelines" and often needs post-processing; deep generative models improve fidelity and diversity. — [Computer-Aided Layout Generation for Building Design: A Review, arXiv 2504.09694](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09694)

### Inferences
- The "barracks" failure is predicted by the literature: an adjacency graph alone does not encode circulation quality, so generators produce reachable-but-poor plans. BuildWise should score the *door graph* (House-GAN++'s functional graph): depth of each room from the entrance, number of rooms reached only via corridor, loop presence, corridor length.
- Useful objective terms drawn from these systems: adjacency satisfaction (graph edit distance to the target bubble diagram), accessibility (every room reachable; private rooms not passed through), room dimension/aspect bounds, boundary fit, and (BuildWise-specific) corridor length/area share.
- Anchor-first generation (fireplace in Prairie; living room in RPLAN; central grid cell in Palladio) is a cheap structural prior: place the hub/living first, then attach zones.

## 5. Layouts by plot shape: narrow/deep vs wide plots; avoiding corridors on narrow plots

### Takeaway
Narrow/deep plots are planned front-to-back (row-house type between party walls) and vertically zoned; light comes from ends, roof and a central lightwell/court, and circulation is pulled to one side or combined with an open stair/void rather than run as a dark central spine. Wide plots allow L/U/courtyard and linked-wing partis with a central hub.

### Cited Findings
- Row house: "rather deep but narrow, laid out between two long party walls and often two levels high"; split-levels often used on narrow lots. — [De Gruyter Building Types taxonomy](https://bdt.degruyter.com/a-taxonomy-of-typological-classifications/)
- Narrow plots: "you cannot widen the building, so you must bring light and air in from above and from a hole in the middle". Daylight reaches ~1.5–2× window head height (~4–5 m); in an 18 m deep house lit only from the ends, a 6–9 m central band gets almost no daylight. Lightwell ~1.2×1.5 m noticeably lights adjoining rooms; a court ≥3×3 m becomes a usable outdoor room; keep the well about as wide as adjoining rooms are tall (≥~2 m for two storeys). A "long dark corridor down the middle" is named as the typical failure; fix by pulling circulation to one side and opening the core; run the stair as an open void with an openable skylight. Side walls are usually blind party walls (openings often barred within side setbacks under ~1 m). Car space ~2.5 m × 4.8–5.5 m; front setbacks ~1.5–3 m (verify locally). — [studiomatrx, Narrow plot design strategies](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/narrow-plot-design-strategies-india) (education blog; illustrative, references outdated NBC 2016)
- Lot width rules of thumb: ~15–18 ft lots → three-storey vertical plans; 18–22 ft → two-storey front-to-back plans; 22–25 ft → optional side circulation; central stair placement said to free ~10–15% usable area. — [Coohom, Best small house layouts for narrow urban lots](https://www.coohom.com/article/best-small-house-layouts-for-narrow-urban-lots) (single low-rigor source; via search summary)
- Stair options on narrow lots: front, middle, side or back; central straight stair, switchback along a party wall, open-tread. — [Houseplanshelper, narrow lots](https://houseplanshelper.com/house-plans-for-narrow-lots.html); [Coohom](https://www.coohom.com/article/best-small-house-layouts-for-narrow-urban-lots) (via search summary)
- Built examples: a Leicester terrace redesign used the staircase to cut across the building, removing dark corridor and landing space ([Architizer, The Etch House](https://architizer.com/projects/the-etch-house)); a house on a 3.7 m wide lot uses a lightwell with a micro-garden visible from the main level ([Architizer, House Contrast](https://architizer.com/projects/house-contrast)); a narrow-lot modern side-hall plan ([Dwell, The Architect Next Door](https://www.dwell.com/article/the-architect-next-door-a1c86c32/6133571393955823616)). (via search summary)
- Linear circulation "is best suited to rectangular or elongated floor plans"; through-room circulation is the most space-efficient linear form. — [Fine Homebuilding](https://finehomebuilding.com/?p=44009)

### Inferences
- Narrow plot rule set for the engine: (a) zone front-to-back (street/public → stair/light core → private/garden) and vertically (public low, private high); (b) put the stair as a cross-cutting element at the middle or along one party wall, doubling as the light/vent void, so it replaces a corridor; (c) side passage along one party wall rather than a central spine; (d) insert a lightwell/court when plot depth exceeds ~2× usable daylight depth (~10 m).
- Wide plot rule set: favour hub house (central hall + living as circulation centre), L (day wing + night wing at a hub), U/courtyard (gallery/veranda around the court as circulation). Bedrooms in their own wing off a short lobby.
- Using plot aspect ratio (depth/width) to choose the parti is a defensible first branch: deep & narrow → row/split-level/lightwell; near-square & wide → hub, courtyard or U; wide & shallow → bar or L with single-loaded gallery on the garden side.

### Gaps
- No authoritative (code or academic) numeric thresholds linking plot width to plan type; the lot-width bands come from one low-rigor source.
- No dimensioned side-passage plans found.
