# Replace corridors with hubs, courts and shade

Around the world, a good modern house comes from a few recurring moves rather than a style. It has **one organising idea you can read at a glance** (a court, an L wrapped round a garden, a core, a pavilion, a wall). Its **wings are narrow enough that every main room gets light and air from two sides**. **Corridors are short or absent**, so people move through a living hub, a lobby or a veranda. Arrival is **staged, from a compressed threshold into a bright main room**. The **public face is closed and the private face open**. The **shaded outdoor rooms are generous**, and the **materials are few, arranged with "ordered complexity"** rather than blankness or clutter. Climate sets the dials. Hot-humid West Africa needs a long east–west axis and deep shade on *both* north and south faces. Nigeria's Building Energy Efficiency Code caps window-to-wall ratio at 20% per façade unless the glazing is shaded and requires roof insulation of R ≥ 1.25 m²K/W. Plans should be cross-ventilated and no deeper than about five times the ceiling height. Households everywhere want similar comforts but arrange them differently. Nigeria's arrangement puts a guest zone at the front (ante room, visitors' lounge, visitors' WC, guest suite), the family lounge deeper in or upstairs, an en-suite to every bedroom and a boys' quarters (BQ) at the back. In practice, creativity means picking one "primary generator" early, testing it, and offering options that really differ. For BuildWise the fix is structural. Generate each option from a named parti and a door graph, not from a hallway with rooms on both sides. Score circulation share, corridor length, two-sided light, the privacy gradient and façade articulation. Add courtyard and cluster plans to the engine's existing hallway, L, pavilion, core and row typologies. The evidence has limits. Most of the numbers come from English and Australian standards and Alexander's *A Pattern Language*. No Nigerian statutory setbacks or room minimums were found. Several thresholds in the rules below are marked as inferences.

## Narrow wings, two-sided light and shade define modern houses

The modernist canon still underlies contemporary houses, but in a changed form. Le Corbusier's Five Points (pilotis, free plan, free façade, ribbon windows, roof garden) all follow from one column grid, and **the free plan is usually treated as the focal point** because it frees partitions from carrying the structure ([Wikipedia: Five Points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier%27s_Five_Points_of_Architecture)). ArchDaily finds each point in recent houses: stilts that free the ground and link public and private space, and long horizontal openings for daylight and panorama ([ArchDaily](https://www.archdaily.com/948273/the-5-points-of-modern-architecture-in-contemporary-projects)). The vocabulary matters less than the physics of light behind it. In *Wings of Light*, Alexander argues for long, narrow wings **never more than about 7.6 m (25 ft) wide and never wider than 9.1 m**, because daylight falls off sharply beyond **3.7–4.6 m** from a window. His cost analysis found the narrowest office plan cost only **about 6% more** than the squarest ([A Pattern Language 107](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl107/apl107.htm)). *Light on Two Sides of Every Room* calls two-sided light perhaps the single pattern that most decides whether a room succeeds. A room lit from one side should be **no more than about 2.4 m deep** unless it is tall, white-walled and lit by high windows. A small house gets two-sided light automatically by putting a room in each corner ([A Pattern Language 159](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl159/apl159.htm)). These two patterns explain better than any style why acclaimed houses keep returning to L, U, courtyard and pavilion forms and avoid deep square boxes.

Current standards have refined "open plan" into **flexible open plan**. London's 2023 Housing Design Standards ask for several things:

- Open-plan living keeps the kitchen adequately separated from the sitting space.
- Homes with three or more bedrooms show how the living space could split into two rooms, each with its own window.
- No living space or habitable room is reached only through the kitchen.
- Homes are dual aspect. Bay windows and shallow recesses do not count as a second aspect.
- Main living rooms and outdoor space get the best orientation, with direct sun and shading.

([GLA Housing Design Standards LPG 2023](https://wandsworth.gov.uk/media/large/Glassmills/CD5/CD5.28_GLA_LPG_Housing_Design_Standards_(2023).pdf))

The same document names cultural requirements explicitly, including "a preference for the kitchen to be separated from the living and dining spaces", so a closed kitchen is a legitimate option, not a lapse. Private outdoor space must be **at least 5 m² for one or two people plus 1 m² per extra bedspace**. It must be **at least 1.5 m deep and wide (2.5 m is best practice)** and open off the sitting room or kitchen/dining room ([GLA LPG 2023](https://wandsworth.gov.uk/media/large/Glassmills/CD5/CD5.28_GLA_LPG_Housing_Design_Standards_(2023).pdf)).

England's Nationally Described Space Standard (NDSS) sets the room-size floors:

- A single bedroom is **7.5 m² and 2.15 m wide**.
- A double bedroom is **11.5 m²**. The first double is **2.75 m wide** and every other double **2.55 m**.
- Ceilings are at least 2.3 m high over 75% of the floor.

([Designing Buildings Wiki: NDSS](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Nationally_described_space_standard))

London raises the ceiling to **2.5 m** and recommends a **3.5 m ground-floor floor-to-floor height**. It requires a combined living/kitchen/dining area of **27–31 m² for 4–6 people**, not counting the space inside the front door or any through-route, and a main sitting space **at least 3.5 m wide** for three or more bedspaces ([GLA LPG 2023](https://wandsworth.gov.uk/media/large/Glassmills/CD5/CD5.28_GLA_LPG_Housing_Design_Standards_(2023).pdf)).

Generous practice sits well above these floors. Typical figures are a master bedroom of **16–20 m²**, a dressing room of at least 1.8 × 1.8 m, an ensuite of **2.9–4.4 m²**, and an island kitchen of about **4.8 × 4.2 m** built from 0.6 m units and 1.2 m aisles. These are practitioner rules of thumb, not a standard ([Homebuilding & Renovating](https://homebuilding.co.uk/room-sizes)).

The section matters as much as the plan. Alexander gives **3.0–3.7 m** ceilings for rooms where many people gather, **2.1–2.7 m** for smaller rooms and **1.8–2.1 m** for alcoves. He cites the traditional Japanese rule that ceiling height grows with floor area, and stresses that variation matters more than absolute height ([A Pattern Language 190](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl190/apl190.htm)).

## Hot-humid West Africa needs shade on both faces and air through every room

Every climate uses the same levers (orientation, shading, ventilation, glazing ratio, thermal mass, insulation, roof form), but sets them differently.

| Climate | What the house does |
|---|---|
| Hot-humid | Shades everything, opens the plan to the breeze, uses light materials and lifts the roof. |
| Hot-dry | Turns inward. Hassan Fathy combined a courtyard, a mashrabiya screen and a wind catcher whose vent faces the prevailing wind, so cooled courtyard air moves through the house ([ResearchGate: Cairene wind catcher](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362712168_Evaluation_of_the_Wind_Catcher_in_the_Traditional_Cairene_Courtyard_Houses'_Integrated_Passive_System_for_Natural_Ventilation_and_Cooling)). |
| Temperate and cold | Consolidates. Passivhaus targets heating demand ≤ 15 kWh/m²·a. It favours compact forms with a low surface-to-volume ratio and total glazing of about **15–20% of treated floor area** ([Passivhaus Trust](https://passivhaustrust.org.uk/UserFiles/File/Technical%20Papers/How%20to%20Build%202023/HowToBuild-Ch7.pdf)). Another guide splits glazing as roughly 10–15% north and 20–30% south ([Levitt Bernstein](https://www.levittbernstein.co.uk/site/assets/files/3553/passivhaus-easi-guide_screen_portrait.pdf)). The two use different bases, so they conflict on paper. |

Orientation therefore has to follow hemisphere and latitude. Near the Equator the sun passes almost overhead, north of the zenith for part of the year and south of it for the rest. In Nigeria that means **both** long faces need overhangs, and east and west are the faces to close.

Tropical modernism turned this climate into form. The V&A sets out its principles:

- Solid east and west walls.
- Long north and south façades open to airflow.
- Brise-soleil screens that let breeze in and keep sun out.
- Wide eaves against sun and rain.
- Adjustable louvres.
- Mono-pitch roofs and long, low blocks.
- Screens patterned with local symbols, as at Opoku Ware School.

([V&A: What is Tropical Modernism?](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/what-is-tropical-modernism))

Koenigsberger's *Manual of Tropical Housing and Building* places the warm-humid belt between the Equator and **about 15°N**. It makes three points for that climate:

- A **separate roof and ceiling** is the natural roof.
- Outdoor spaces matter even more than in hot-dry regions.
- Walls and roofs should have low thermal capacity and good insulation.

([Manual of Tropical Housing, Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/470790221/Otto-H-Koenigsberger-Manual-of-Tropical-Housing-and-Building))

Kéré's Gando Primary School gives the clearest contemporary version. A raised metal roof floats over a perforated clay ceiling, and the interior stays **up to 6 °C cooler** than under tin alone ([Aga Khan Award](https://www.akdn.org/architecture/project/primary-school)). Every classroom has openings at both ends for cross-ventilation ([ArchDaily](https://www.archdaily.com/978851/how-does-francis-kere-use-materials-to-respond-to-local-climate-conditions/)). Bawa's courts and verandas and Correa's open-to-sky and tube houses apply the same logic in South Asia ([RTF on Bawa](https://www.re-thinkingthefuture.com/know-your-architects/a14954-blending-nature-and-vernacular-geoffrey-bawas-tropical-modernism/); [studioMATRX](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/tropical-architecture-india)). Curators note that single-room-deep, shaded, cross-ventilated plans existed in the region centuries before Fry and Drew ([e-flux](https://www.e-flux.com/architecture//552588/contested-symbols-of-coloniality-and-nation-building/)).

These sources yield a handful of hard numbers.

- **Nigeria's Building Energy Efficiency Code (2017):**
  - "Window to Wall Ratio for any orientation shall not exceed 20%". Where that cannot be met, all glazing on the façade must be adequately shaded.
  - Every roof needs insulation of **R ≥ 1.25 m²K/W**.
  - Residential lighting power is capped at **6 W/m²**.

  ([BEEC PDF](https://engineersforum.com.ng/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Building-Energy-Efficiency-Code.pdf))
- **Ventilation depth:** a room can be up to **5× its ceiling height deep** if cross-ventilated, and **2.5×** if ventilated from one side only ([Essex Design Guide](https://www.essexdesignguide.co.uk/climate-change/solar-orientation/natural-ventilation/); [Designing Buildings Wiki](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Cross_ventilation)). The rule is widely attributed to CIBSE, but no CIBSE source confirmed it.
- **Eaves:** Australia's YourHome sizes eaves at about **45% of the height from window sill to eave underside**. Its table gives 450 mm for 0.9–1.2 m of height, 600 mm for 1.2–1.35 m, 900 mm for 1.35–2.1 m and 1,200 mm for 2.1–2.7 m. It adds that warmer climates need more shade and that west faces need vertical screens, because low afternoon sun gets under any eave ([YourHome: Shading](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/passive-design/shading)).

That eave rule was written for winter-sun climates, so in the tropics treat it as a minimum. A 500 mm shading depth attributed to the Nigerian code could not be found in the code text ([JEA Lokoja study](https://jea.thebrpi.org/journals/jea/Vol_7_No_1_June_2019/6.pdf)).

There is also a live tension. A research proposal argues for **at least 25%** window-to-wall ratio and windows at least 1,350 mm wide to help hot-humid ventilation ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/395424729_Reevaluating_Window_Design_Standards_in_Nigerian_Architecture_Optimizing_Width_1350mm_and_Ventilation_Efficiency_for_Hot-Humid_Climates)). The code's own shading route resolves it: **large openings are acceptable when they are shaded**.

The Nigerian market has moved away from this logic. Osasona describes the typical contemporary house: two storeys of sandcrete blockwork with air conditioning, casement windows in place of louvres, and the deep eaves gone. The result is overheating and dependence on air conditioning. The vernacular features worth reviving are **deep eaves, louvres, impluvium courtyards, verandas and raised floors** ([Buildings & Cities](https://buildingsandcities.org/insights/commentaries/sustainable-homes-nigeria.html)).

Critics go further. One says about 95% of new developments look like "white boxes" ([The Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/?p=2949777)). Architects warn that small decorative windows now sacrifice light and airflow ([The Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/property/climate-concerns-trail-growing-preference-for-compact-window-designs/)). An opinion piece says breeze-block screens and courtyards have been cut to maximise sellable floor area ([Realtor Kingz](https://realtorkingz.com/lost-arts-of-nigerian-architecture/)).

The best-documented local counter-example is Tosin Oshinowo. Her practice puts passive cooling first. Her Lantern House in Lagos (911 m², five bedrooms) reinterprets the **layered public-to-private organisation of Yoruba family life**, with a screened terrace acting as a private courtyard ([ArchDaily](https://www.archdaily.com/1024037/tosin-oshinowo-redefining-african-architecture-through-context-and-collaboration); [ArchDaily: Lantern House](https://www.archdaily.com/999044/the-lantern-house-cmdesign-atelier)). The upper market's taste for interlocking, cantilevered volumes is documented only in firm marketing ([elArc Designs](https://elarcdesigns.com/projects-item/burna-boy-lekki-home/)). That taste and the climate logic can be reconciled when **upper floors overhang to shade the floor below** (inference).

## Architects plan along an intimacy gradient, not down a corridor

Architects zone a house before drawing walls. They tag each room as **public, private or service**, then record adjacencies in a matrix on a scale such as essential (2), desirable (1), neutral (0) or keep apart (−). The strong daily pairs are **kitchen–dining** and a **step-free link from the master bedroom to its bathroom**. The entrance should filter guests away from private zones ([illustrarch: bubble diagrams](https://illustrarch.com/articles/architectural-diagrams/98402-bubble-diagram-residential-layout.html); [CUNY City Tech programming assignment](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/arch3612f23/assignments/assignment-programing/)).

Alexander's *Intimacy Gradient* orders these zones. The domestic sequence runs gate, porch, entrance, common space and kitchen, then private rooms. Common rooms sit at the heart of the house, and **paths to private rooms pass alongside them, not through them**. Bathrooms sit halfway between common and private areas. In Peruvian houses some guests never get past the formal *sala*, and merging the *sala* into the family room "draws complaints" because it breaks the gradient ([A Pattern Language 127](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl127/apl127.htm)). That is a precise description of the Nigerian visitors' lounge.

Alexander's guidance on circulation is blunt: "As far as possible, avoid the use of corridors and passages." Common rooms should carry movement **in a chain or loop**, with private rooms opening directly off them. Rooms used for movement should have more than one door, placed in the corners. Any unavoidable passage should be wide, bright and "ideally on one side of the building" ([A Pattern Language 131](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl131/apl131.htm)). Long sterile corridors "represent bureaucracy and monotony". Passages begin to feel dead beyond **about 15 m (50 ft)**, and the best have windows along a whole wall ([A Pattern Language 132](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl132/apl132.htm)).

Fine Homebuilding ranks the options this way:

- Circulation through rooms is the most space-efficient.
- Single-loaded corridors cost area but buy privacy.
- Double-loaded corridors "can feel disorienting".
- Every path should have a window on at least one side.
- Dead ends should be avoided, so traffic can loop.

([Fine Homebuilding: Houses that Flow](https://finehomebuilding.com/?p=44009))

The De Gruyter typology lists the corridor-free alternatives by name: **Inserted Core**, **Living Room as Circulation Center** ("the intersection of all routes") and **Circular Path** ([De Gruyter Building Types](https://bdt.degruyter.com/a-taxonomy-of-typological-classifications/)).

No standards body publishes a circulation share for single-family houses. The practitioners' rule of thumb is **10–15% of floor area, with more than 20% a sign that space can be reclaimed** ([Studio Matrx](https://www.studiomatrx.org/students/small-space-design/circulation-and-flow); [floorplan-ai docs](https://floorplan-ai-docs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/layout-guidance.html)). Published Houzz examples range from 32% down to 4% ([GardenWeb/Houzz](https://www.gardenweb.com/ideabooks/17320457/list/do-you-really-need-that-hallway)). The NDSS shows that a stair costs about **6–7 m² per extra storey** ([GLA LPG Table A1.1](https://wandsworth.gov.uk/media/large/Glassmills/CD5/CD5.28_GLA_LPG_Housing_Design_Standards_(2023).pdf)).

There is a fair counter-argument. Sarah Susanka advises *against* shrinking entries and passages, because generous circulation makes a small house feel larger ([Christian Science Monitor](https://proof.csmonitor.com/1998/0819/081998.feat.feat.2.html)). London's standards likewise want at least half of a home's storage placed off circulation spaces ([GLA LPG 2023](https://wandsworth.gov.uk/media/large/Glassmills/CD5/CD5.28_GLA_LPG_Housing_Design_Standards_(2023).pdf)). The target, then, is not minimal circulation but **circulation that is also a place**: a lit lobby, a landing with a seat, or a hall lined with storage.

Generative-design research predicts the "barracks" failure exactly. House-GAN takes a bubble diagram whose edges mean adjacency, not doors. Its documented failures are rooms with the wrong size or shape and rooms with blocked or missing entries ([House-GAN](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.06988)). House-GAN++ switched to a **door-connectivity graph** and generates doors ([House-GAN++](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.02574)). RPLAN reportedly places the **living room first** and adds rooms around it ([DeepLayout project](https://ustc-gcl-f.github.io/projects/DeepLayout/index.html)). The Prairie-house grammar begins from the fireplace ([Koning & Eizenberg 1981](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/b080295)). A generator that places rooms first and then threads a hallway through them will produce reachable but poor plans. One that **places the hub first and scores the door graph** will not.

The entrance is its own sequence. Entering straight from the street gives no sense of arrival. Alexander's *Entrance Transition* asks for a change of **light, level, surface, direction and above all view** between street and door. In one housing study about 90% of residents said their roughly 6 m front gardens were the right size or too small, though only 15% sat in them ([A Pattern Language 112](https://patterns-dev.github.io/patterns/clean2/apl112/apl112.htm)). Inside, the established technique is **compression and release** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_and_expansion)). The Robie House entry is at most about 2.1 m high before it opens to long, light-filled views ([Houseplans.com](https://www.houseplans.com/blog/wrights-robie-house-the-first-modern-family-home)). Villa Mairea goes from a canopy to a small lobby, then down four steps into an open hall ([Wikipedia: Villa Mairea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Mairea)). Barragán leads from a "bare and dim foyer" into a high, yellow-lit vestibule ([Wikipedia: Barragán House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Barrag%C3%A1n_House_and_Studio)). Farnsworth makes the visitor turn 90° on floating steps ([Wikipedia: Farnsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farnsworth_House)).

## Six plan families cover twenty-seven acclaimed houses

A **parti** (from the French *parti pris*, a decision taken) is the one organising idea of a building, drawn as a one-line diagram such as "rooms around a courtyard" ([Wikipedia: Parti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parti_(architecture))). The research notes examined 27 acclaimed houses from 1928 to 2026. Every one of them falls into one of six parti families: **pavilion or slab around a core, L, courtyard, cluster of volumes, bar or linear bays, and vertical stack or split-level**. Each family pairs predictably with a circulation strategy and an outdoor relationship.

| House | Parti | How people move | Outdoor relationship | Lesson for an engine |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Farnsworth (Mies, 1951), ≈150 m² | Glass pavilion around an off-centre 4.0 × 7.6 m service core | No corridors; furniture zones the space | Glass on all sides, floating terrace | Wrap living around a core ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farnsworth_House)) |
| Stahl House (Koenig, 1960), ≈200 m² | L wrapping a pool, living wing plus bedroom wing | "There are no hallways" | Every room except service faces the pool; 2.1–2.4 m eaves | Day wing and night wing hinged at a hub ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stahl_House)) |
| Villa Mairea (Aalto, 1939) | L around a lawn and pool | Canopy, lobby, steps down to the hall; guest corridor single-loaded | Garden held in the angle | The only corridor is single-loaded and fully glazed ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Mairea)) |
| Fallingwater (Wright, 1936–39) | Cruciform, terraces cantilevered from a stone core | Low halls release into big rooms | 227 m² of terraces against 268 m² inside | Outdoor area nearly equal to indoor ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallingwater)) |
| Eames House (1949), 230 m² | Two pavilions in a line with a court between | Double-height living room with a one-storey alcove | Meadow and a row of trees kept open | Split the programme into pavilions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eames_House)) |

| Compact House, Accra (Mustard), 40 × 80 ft plot | "A mini version of the courtyard compound" | Open-plan ground floor, three bedrooms upstairs | Court with a lap pool | The West African compound shrunk to an urban plot ([Wallpaper*](https://www.wallpaper.com/architecture/residential/compact-house-alice-asafu-adjaye-accra-ghana)) |

Five lessons cut across these houses (synthesis of the cited cases):

- **None of the classics has a long, dark, double-loaded corridor.** Movement is either removed altogether (Farnsworth, Stahl, the Glass House, Butantã) or staged as an experience (Savoye's ramp, Sumiyoshi's court).
- **Outdoor area rivals indoor area.** Stahl's roof covers about 370 m² over roughly 200 m² of interior.
- **Size does not make these houses acclaimed.** Most single-family exemplars are 65–280 m².
- **Contemporary work fragments the house** into volumes with gardens between them.
- **Recent awards reward one bold move on an ordinary site.** The RIBA's 2023 winner was a Tottenham terrace remade around an atrium, which the jury called "extraordinary design within an ordinary context" ([Wallpaper*](https://www.wallpaper.com/architecture/riba-house-of-the-year-2023-winner)).

Plot shape decides which family fits. A row house is "rather deep but narrow, laid out between two long party walls" ([De Gruyter](https://bdt.degruyter.com/a-taxonomy-of-typological-classifications/)). On such plots one widely repeated guide gives these rules:

- Daylight reaches only about 4–5 m in from each end.
- A **long dark corridor down the middle** is the typical failure.
- The fix is to pull circulation to one side and run the stair as an open, skylit void.
- A lightwell of about 1.2 × 1.5 m noticeably helps the rooms beside it.
- A court of **3 × 3 m or more** becomes a usable outdoor room.

The guide is an education blog, so treat these as illustrative ([studiomatrx: narrow plots](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/narrow-plot-design-strategies-india)). Wider plots allow L, U and courtyard plans. Introverted courtyard houses often use the court itself as circulation ([McGill thesis](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/x059c9526.pdf)). The L addresses privacy and noise and defines a garden ([The Plan Collection](https://www.theplancollection.com/blog/4-advantages-of-l-shaped-house-plans-and-how-they-solve-common-problems)).

## Creativity means one strong idea, tested and varied

Studies of practising architects contradict the textbook sequence of analysis followed by synthesis. Jane Darke found that architects "latch on to a relatively simple idea very early", which she called the **primary generator**. They then sketch a crude design around it and test it. Lawson extends this into a negotiation between problem and solution, and notes that generators are not always single ([Lawson, *How Designers Think*](https://www.academia.edu/1905966/How_Designers_Think)).

Concept design "is seldom a 'Eureka!' moment". Architects typically "generate several alternative strategies for developing a site", weigh them with the client and agree a way forward ([Designing Buildings Wiki](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Concept_architectural_design)). Computational practice formalises the same habit. It generates a spectrum of options, scores each against several criteria ([US Patent 11409920](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11409920)) and enforces a **diversity metric** so that the options form genuinely different clusters ([IDEA thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/9342443.pdf)).

Ching's principle of **transformation** supplies the grammar for variants. A concept "can be altered through a series of discrete manipulations and permutations … without loss of identity" ([Designing Buildings Wiki: Principles of Order](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Principles_of_Order_in_Architecture)). In practice that means mirroring an L, extruding a wing, inserting a court or lifting a pavilion, while the parti stays the same.

Rule-breaking has its own theory. Venturi favours "both-and" elements that hold two contradictory characteristics at once, and grounds that richness in hierarchy ([Mount Holyoke blog on Venturi](https://commons.mtholyoke.edu/architectualblog/?p=2)). Ochshorn counters that complex geometry does not reflect the complexity of life ([Agathón 2024](https://doaj.org/article/41ac39b40cbd4f0ba5728eb631517e98)). The exemplars back a middle path. Each acclaimed house can be named in a sentence ("Live with the waterfall", "an oasis behind a blank wall", "one wall organises the site"), and its creativity lies in applying that sentence completely, not in adding forms. The notes found no source on constraint as a driver of creativity. The tight-plot award winners suggest it, but that remains unsourced.

## Ordered complexity, not ornament or blankness, reads as beautiful

**Classical tools.** Ching canonised six ordering principles: **axis, symmetry, hierarchy, datum, rhythm and transformation**. On hierarchy, a dominant form is "significantly different in size", shape or location ([Ching, ch. 7](https://archive.org/download/ArchitectureFormSpaceAndOrderCh7/Architecture_Form%20Space%20and%20Order_Ch7.pdf)). Palladio's seven "most beautiful" room shapes are usually read as ratios of **1:1, √2:1, 4:3, 3:2, 5:3 and 2:1** ([Nexus Network Journal](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-022-00592-1)). Mairea's dining room is a double square ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Mairea)). An analysis of Le Corbusier's regulating lines at Savoye finds he applied them "selectively" ([Issuu student essay](https://issuu.com/atehrani/docs/ideal_villa_2)). Proportion works as a soft discipline, not a law.

**Planning codes.** Codes translate the classical tools into rules:

- The base–middle–top division dates from Chicago skyscrapers ([Chicago Architecture Center](https://architecture.org/online-resources/architecture-encyclopedia/tripartite-division)). It is required for three or more storeys, but **buildings of two storeys or fewer do not need a middle** ([Iowa City code](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/iowacityia/latest/iowacity_ia/0-0-0-30946)).
- Windows should sit **at least 75 mm (3 in) deep** so the façade does not look flat ([Milwaukee design guidelines](https://city.milwaukee.gov/Designguidelines/UrbanDesignResources/UrbanDesignBuildingDevelopmentTypes/Multi-Family-Residential.htm)).
- The UK model design code suggests glazing "might be limited to 35%" outside city centres and lets codes set the depth of reveals ([DLUHC fact sheet](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6543b9941f1a600010360ce8/Fact_sheet_6._Sustainable_design_and_placemaking.pdf)). That figure is unverified against the full code.

**What looks institutional.** Codes agree on the cause and the cure. The cause is "long facades lacking variety or physical breaks" ([Merri-bek](https://www.merri-bek.vic.gov.au/globalassets/website-merri-bek/areas/building-business/planning-and-building/planning/the-good-design-advice-sheets---2.-exterior-appearance.pdf)). The cure is articulation by **porches, projections, bay windows, recesses and wall-plane changes** ([Lakemoor code](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/lakemoor/latest/lakemoor_il/0-0-0-4606); [ecode360](https://ecode360.com/37227737)). Walls should be "neither monotonous nor overly complex" ([ecode360](https://ecode360.com/37227613)). Adjacent buildings should vary their setback by at least **0.6 m (2 ft)** ([Sacramento County](https://landuse.saccounty.gov/dg/ch3/)).

**Preference research.** A systematic review of 61 studies finds that **complexity, materiality, symmetry and greenery** consistently predict emotional response to façades ([Wang, Ding & Gath-Morad 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22599v1)). Within those studies:

- **Complexity follows an inverted U**: moderate complexity is preferred, and excess visual load lowers valence.
- Eye-tracking shows that *order within complexity*, not density, drives preference.
- **Symmetrical, regular windows raise valence**, so regularity in itself is not what looks institutional.
- Greenery on buildings is rated restorative and beautiful.
- Featureless or reflective surfaces lower ratings.

Measured visual attraction was higher for postmodern houses than for modern or neo-modern ones ([Lee & Ostwald 2023 summary](https://housingandcommunityregeneration.nd.edu/research/the-visual-attractiveness-of-architectural-facades-measuring-visual-complexity-and-attractive-strength-in-architecture/)). UK surveys find the public prefers detail, "variety within a coherent pattern" and textured materials ([RIBA Journal](https://www.ribaj.com/intelligence/public-opinion-architecture-why-numbers-matter/)).

**What reads as institutional, in one sentence.** The institutional or barracks look is **length without breaks, flatness without shadow, and repetition without a dominant element**, not regularity or plainness as such.

**Materials, colour and roofs.** The exemplars use a small palette, often local, plus one strong accent: Barragán's single fuchsia walls, the Eames panels, Fallingwater's red steel, the green stair in the RIBA 2023 house ([Wikipedia: Barragán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Barrag%C3%A1n_House_and_Studio); [Wikipedia: Eames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eames_House)). The common practitioner rule of two or three exterior materials could not be traced to a source.

The roofscape divides the same way. Acclaimed houses use either deep horizontal eaves (Stahl's 2.1–2.4 m, Farnsworth's 1.7 m cantilever) or a single expressive roof (Magney's thin curve, Kéré's floating sheet roof). Nigeria's critics, by contrast, describe "fortresses … monoliths of blockwork and high-pitched roofs" ([Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/?p=2651258)), and Osasona traces the extra-steep roof with cornice eaves to British roofing tradition ([Buildings & Cities](https://buildingsandcities.org/insights/commentaries/sustainable-homes-nigeria.html)).

**The senses.** Pallasmaa adds a non-visual dimension. Touch, sound and smell belong in the experience of a building, a claim critics accept even while disputing his wider theory ([UCL Press](https://journals.uclpress.co.uk/amps/article/pubid/Archit_MPS-27-2/)).

**Gaps.** No West African façade-preference study was found, so all of this is imported evidence.

## Households want the same comforts arranged differently

Nigeria's programme is well defined.

**The duplex.** Nigeria Property Centre describes the typical duplex. The ground floor holds the sitting and dining rooms, kitchen, guest toilet and sometimes a guest bedroom. The upper floor holds the main bedrooms. The BQ is "usually a separate small building at the back of the compound" ([Nigeria Property Centre](https://nigeriapropertycentre.com/glossary/duplex)). A Lagos architect's four-bedroom duplex adds detail:

- Ground floor: main lounge, visitors' toilet, kitchen, dining and an en-suite guest bedroom.
- First floor: **family lounge**, master suite and two more en-suite rooms, each with a terrace.
- A void over the main lounge.
- A roof terrace.

([Archinect](https://archinect.com/Olateju-Joshua-Abodunrin/project/4-bedroom-duplex))

**What residents want.** A survey of 2,133 Abuja civil servants found strong preferences for **dining spaces, en-suite bedrooms, stores, ante rooms and visitors' toilets**. Laundries, studies, prayer rooms and toilets separate from bedrooms were wanted less, and income shaped most preferences ([Akewusola & Firdaus 2026](https://journals.uniosun.edu.ng/ujees/article/view/492)). In Ibadan public housing, **50.38% of residents rated their privacy low or very low**. Visual privacy scored worst, and the study blamed the placement of rooms and openings and the lack of a guest room ([Amao & Ilesanmi 2022](https://doaj.org/article/d60f184b4c854a61871cd197fbec0e5f)).

**What the plot allows.** A Lagos "full plot" is quoted as 648 m² (18 × 36 m). Outside Lagos, 50 × 100 ft (about 464 m²) is common ([Nigeria Property Centre](https://nigeriapropertycentre.com/glossary/plot-of-land)). Lagos caps the building footprint at **50%, 60% and 70%** of the plot for low, medium and high density. Surveyed medium-density estates actually reached about 70% ([Fadera & Olaniyi 2022](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/ahr-2022-0008.pdf)).

**Services.** Boreholes are a necessity where public water is unreliable ([The Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/?p=2824594)).

**What the notes did not find.** No citable residential setbacks, generator or inverter rooms, or outdoor-kitchen conventions turned up, so the engine should not hard-code them.

**Other markets.** The other markets differ along four axes: whether there is a formal guest zone, whether the kitchen is open or closed, whether there are staff quarters, and orientation rules.

| Market | Guest zone | Kitchen | Staff | Orientation and other |
|---|---|---|---|---|

**The cross-market pattern.** The Gulf dirty kitchen, Australia's butler's pantry, China's enclosed kitchen and Mexico's service kitchen all answer one need: **keep smoke, mess and staff work out of the room where guests are received**. A single option, a show kitchen in front with a service kitchen behind it, would cover most of these markets (inference).

## Flats, schools, clinics and halls scale up the same lessons

The non-house guidance repeats the house lessons at a larger scale: shallow plans, light and air from two sides, short or open-air circulation, a legible entrance and one dominant element.

### Apartments

The NSW Apartment Design Guide (ADG) and London's 2023 standards set these numbers:

| Topic | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Block depth | **12–18 m glass to glass**; a cross-through flat ≤ 18 m deep | ADG |
| Single-aspect rooms | Depth ≤ 2.5 × ceiling height; open-plan living ≤ 8 m from a window | ADG |
| Windows | In an external wall, glass ≥ 10% of the room's floor area; no borrowed light | ADG |
| Flats per core | **At most 8 per floor** | ADG and London |
| Flats per lift | At most 40 in buildings of 10 storeys or more | ADG |
| Corridors | Articulated with windows and seating bays when **longer than 12 m from the lift core** | ADG |
| Cross-ventilation | **At least 60% of flats** in the first nine storeys | ADG |
| Balconies | **2 m deep (2.4 m for three bedrooms)**; 8–12 m² | ADG |
| Ceilings | 2.7 m in habitable rooms; 3.3 m on mixed-use ground and first floors | ADG |
| Floor-to-floor allowance | Ceiling height plus 0.4 m | ADG |
| Corridor width and access | Corridors ≥ 1.5 m; open access decks preferred over internal corridors because they make flats dual aspect | London |

([NSW ADG Part 4](https://www.planning.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-03/apartment-design-guide-part-4-designing-the-building.pdf); [NSW ADG Part 2](https://www.planning.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-03/apartment-design-guide-part-2-developing-the-controls.pdf); [GLA LPG 2023](https://wandsworth.gov.uk/media/large/Glassmills/CD5/CD5.28_GLA_LPG_Housing_Design_Standards_(2023).pdf))

The ADG's façade toolkit is a ready-made checklist:

- A defined base, middle and top.
- Floors grouped on tall buildings.
- Corners given prominence.
- Shadow from balconies and deeper reveals.

The award-winning precedents get their character from entrances and outdoor space, not ornament:

- **McGrath Road** (Peter Barber): 26 homes with no internal communal circulation; every home has its own street or courtyard door and a roof terrace ([Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2019/03/25/peter-barber-architects-mcgrath-road-housing-london/amp/)).
- **Grand Parc Bordeaux** (Lacaton & Vassal): about 3.8 m of winter gardens and balconies added to 530 flats ([Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2021/03/17/key-projects-anne-lacaton-jean-philippe-vassal-pritzker-prize/amp/)).
- **Pinnacle@Duxton**: sky gardens on the 26th and 50th floors linking seven towers ([ULI](https://casestudies.uli.org/the-pinnacleduxton)).

### Offices, shops and mixed use

- **Office depth.** Daylight is "nearly impossible" beyond about **7.6 m from one window wall** ([Advanced Buildings Pattern 3](https://patternguide.advancedbuildings.net/patterns/pattern-3-section-depth)). Office wings lit from both sides therefore work at about 12–15 m deep (inference).
- **Shop frontage.** Lively frontage means **15–20 units per 100 m** ([Gehl, Seattle](https://issuu.com/gehlarchitects/docs/565_seattle_pspl/58)), or at least 10 doors per 100 m ([City at Eye Level](https://thecityateyelevel.com/stories/close-encounters-with-buildings/)).
- **Mixed-use section.** A deeper commercial podium sits under a narrower residential block, for example 18 m of housing over 21.6 m of commercial space ([NSW ADG Part 2](https://www.planning.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-03/apartment-design-guide-part-2-developing-the-controls.pdf)).

### Schools

- **UK standard designs.** The UK government's baseline schools are **finger blocks**. Primary classrooms are **7.2 m deep and lit from both sides**, and secondary teaching runs are 7.8 m deep. Voids act as ventilation stacks ([DfE 240-place primary](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/baseline-design-240-place-primary-school-with-a-26-place-nursery); [DfE 1,850-place secondary](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/baseline-design-1850-place-secondary-school)).
- **Gando.** Kéré's school adds the double roof and wide eaves described above.

### Clinics

- **Butaro District Hospital** (MASS Design Group, Rwanda) is a campus of linked buildings around a traditional gathering tree. **Covered external corridors** run along the building edges, wards face the landscape, and high ceilings, louvres and fans drive natural ventilation ([MASS Design Group](https://modelofarchitecture.org/work/design/butaro-district-hospital)).
- **Léo** (Kéré) arranges ten modules "like houses in a village" along a central street ([Kéré Architecture](https://www.kerearchitecture.com/work/building/surgical-clinic-and-health-centre)).

### Hotels

- Guest floors make up 65–85% of a hotel's area, and the double-loaded slab is the usual type ([Design Guide for Hotels](https://www.scribd.com/doc/269116558/7151503-Design-Guide-for-Hotels)).
- Rooms are planned on a **3.6 m module** in budget hotels and **4.2–4.5 m** at the upper end ([Eco-resorts guide](https://issuu.com/sulmankhalid/docs/eco-resorts.planning.and.design.for/151)).
- Both sources are secondary. A hotel's character therefore has to come from its base, court and roof.

### Worship and event buildings

- **Bait Ur Rouf Mosque** (Marina Tabassum, Dhaka) sits on a raised plinth with no dome or minaret. Light wells, a colonnade and perforated brick light a column-free prayer hall ([Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2017/03/05/bait-ur-rouf-mosque-dhaka-bangladesh-marina-tabassum-brick-aga-khan-award/amp/)).
- **La Ascensión del Señor** (AGi Architects): a church roof that "unfolds freely" to cover the assembly and bring in light ([ArchDaily](https://www.archdaily.com/445306/la-ascension-del-senor-church-agi-architects/)).
- **Event halls:** no numeric guidance was found. They are best treated as worship's secular twin: forecourt, covered threshold, clear-span hall, expressive roof.

| Plot condition | Preferred parti, in order | Why |
|---|---|---|

| Near-square, medium (e.g. 18 × 36 m Lagos plot, 50 × 100 ft) | Hub house (two-storey with an upstairs family-lounge hub); L with garden in the angle; compact court house | Nigerian duplex pattern; Stahl and Mairea; Accra Compact House |
| Wide or large (≥ ~25 m frontage, e.g. 30 × 30 m estate plots) | U or courtyard with a veranda gallery round the court; linked pavilions (day and night) with a covered walkway; cluster of blocks with gardens | Bawa, House for Trees, Palmyra, Eames |
| Wide and shallow | Single bar facing the breeze, with a single-loaded gallery on the garden side; or an L | Pattern 107; tropical modernist long low blocks |
| Bungalow brief on any plot | L or pavilion before hallway; hub only if the plot is too small for wings | Wings keep bedroom routes short and private |

### Prompt guidance for the AI designer and critique

The following text is written to be added to `apps/api/src/ai/designer-prompt.ts`; bump `DESIGNER_PROMPT_VERSION`. A shorter form fits the critique instructions.

```text
DESIGN METHOD
1. Choose ONE parti for each option and state it in one sentence before any room:
   hub house, L around a garden, U or courtyard house, linked pavilions, cluster of
   blocks, single bar facing the breeze, narrow plan with a light-void stair, or
   vertical stack. Different options must use different partis.
2. Place the hub first (living room, court or core), then attach zones along a privacy
   gradient: covered porch -> ante lobby -> visitors' lounge + guest WC -> dining,
   kitchen, family lounge -> bedroom lobby or upstairs family lounge -> bedrooms.
   Paths to bedrooms run beside common rooms, never across the visitors' lounge or
   through the kitchen.
3. Circulation: avoid corridors. Use the living hub, small lobbies with doors round
   their edge, a veranda gallery round a court, or a covered link between pavilions.
   No straight passage over 6 m; any passage over 4 m has a window; never a dark
   central spine; never more than 2 bedroom doors facing each other along a passage.
   Keep circulation under 15% of floor area.
4. Light and air: every habitable room on an outside wall; living room and master
   bedroom with windows on two walls. Wings 7.6 m deep or less where possible
   (9.1 m max). Room depth from a single window wall no more than 2.5 x ceiling height.
5. Climate (hot-humid): long axis east-west; stores, WCs and stairs on east and west
   ends; deep eaves or canopies (0.9 m or more) on BOTH north and south faces;
   vertical screens on west glazing; any large glazing must be shaded; ventilated roof
   over an insulated ceiling.
6. Arrival: covered porch at least 1.5 m deep, a step up, one turn between gate and
   door, a lower lobby, then a view through the living room to the garden or court.
7. Outdoors: a terrace, veranda or court off the living room or kitchen, at least
   2.5 m in its smaller dimension.
8. Facade: closed, screened face to the street, open shaded face to the garden. One
   dominant element per elevation, marked entrance, a plane change of at least 0.6 m
   every 8-10 m, windows on shared head and sill lines, 2-3 materials plus one accent.
   Not a blank box, not a collage.
9. Concept story: name the parti, the arrival, the outdoor room, and at least one
   non-visual quality (shade, breeze, rain on the veranda roof, texture underfoot).
```

The critique step can check the snapshot against six questions:

1. Does any elevation run more than about 10 m without a break?
2. Is the entrance unmarked?
3. Are the windows flush or scattered off any datum?
4. Is there a long passage visible in plan?
5. Do the large openings lack shade?
6. Do the four options read as the same building?

The critique should return a corrected layout only when one of these fails.

## Conclusion

The barracks plan is mainly a representation problem, not a styling one. An engine that sees a house as rooms plus an adjacency list will always reach for a hallway to connect them. The research, from Alexander to House-GAN++, says the house must be generated as a parti with a hub and scored as a door graph. Once that changes, most aesthetic gains follow almost for free: two-sided light, short routes, a staged arrival and an outdoor room per wing. A façade score with an inverted-U complexity term then guards against both the white box and the collage.

The most useful finding for BuildWise's worked example is that the "modern" option and the "Nigerian" option largely coincide. The impluvium court, the veranda, the ante room, the visitors' parlour and the separate BQ are the same moves that acclaimed houses worldwide make with courts, galleries, entry lobbies, intimacy gradients and pavilions. They are also what the Lagos market has lost to sellable floor area and blank blockwork. A generator that offers a shaded courtyard or pavilion duplex, with a proper privacy gradient and both faces under deep eaves, is not imposing foreign taste. It is restoring local logic in a form the market already admires. The unsourced thresholds above should be treated as starting values for the assistant's own feedback loop, which can calibrate them against what Nigerian households actually choose.
